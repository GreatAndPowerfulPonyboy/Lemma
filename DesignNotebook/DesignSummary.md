# Design summary

## Changes from initial concept design : Problem Framing
My original problem domain was too focused on comparables, being that of flashcarding/spaced-repetition more generally, but it soon shifted to self-regulated learning for technical subjects specifically. This shift was made to focus more on the bad situations that I cared without, which was my personal inability to find a satisfying way to leverage Spaced Repetition for my technical subjects, as well as dissatisfaction with the way I psetted. From this domain shift came a completely different app idea by the name of ReDerive, which was going to be a mixture of spaced-repetition and guided meditation for problem setting. The idea was to have a system that would help prod a user to create variants on the problems they solved for class and explain each step of their thought process in order to bolster their understanding, but I found myself stuck ruminating on the concepts necessary to achieve this. Much of this rumination was due to a lack of belief that what I had at the time was capable of being useful. What I ought to have done, I know now, was createa paper prototype of the system and get some friends to try it out so I didn't waste time in my own head, but that's a lesson for later.
Moving off of ReDerive, I went back to a slightly more generic spaced-repetition app, but I kept some of that technical subject focus by zeroing in on the elaboration of prompts instead of the creation of cards.
## Changes from initial concept design: Concepts

### Removal and Re-adding of Prompting
The most important change from my P1 design was the splitting apart of the Prompting concept into Cueing and Flashcarding. I realized the concept had to be decomposed because a lot of my code theorycrafting involved special casing a prompt based on whether it was a flashcard or a cue, which clued me into the fact that I was consolidating two different courses of behaviour. While a cue and a flashcard are both 'prompts', a cue only really makes sense within the context of trying to explain some topic. Like a cue card, one uses it to give themselves a brief jog of memory as they explain something. On the other hand, a flashcard makes sense as its own unit of review due to having a question to provide context and an answer to have something to grade against. Thus, Prompting was carrying with it two different purposes and was split into Cueing and Flashcarding.
In the MVP of the app, prompts were removed as a concept because the unit of review was a topic and the points within it. My thought process here was that I wanted to simplify review scheduling because I was worried that allowing users to create prompts that reused points would lead to eyes glazing over during review sessions if multiple prompst were due on the same day that featured the same points. By P3, I decided to revert this change bbecause while playing around with the app to study for some of my own classes, I was finding it very restrictive that I couldn't break down a topic into different prompts to test my ability to explain things at different levels of granularity. Since I find the ability to do this integral to the app's user fantasy, I moved to prompts being the unit of review. However, this was not done under a Prompting concept, because at
that point in the design there was no realized course of behavior that prompting offered users that wasn't identical to having RecallChecking be what was scheduled by SpacedRepetition. As a result, prompts shifted to being a frontend harness of sorts for RecallChecking.

### Removal of Tagging
With the reintroducing of prompting to the application under RecallChecking, I also found that tags were no longer an essential part of the design. By grouping a set of points into a recall check, a user was already able to create a cross-topic unit of review. As a result, while tagging would've provided functionality for users in the ability to create a very coarse-grained selection of points without the restriction of every point either having to be 'unsorted' or within a topic, I found it wasn't worth the additional testing, reaction checking, and other dev work necessary. Of course, this could be a bout of narrow-mindedness on my part, and user testing could reveal a desire for it, especially as a different way to filter points on the main screen, like in my original User Journey.

### Loosening Categorizing's restriction
Originally, every point had to be in a topic, which was a Categorizing category. This was another vestigal restriction of topics being a unit of review from the MVP. When topics were the review unit, I wanted users to be able to cover every point they have made by reviewing all their topics in order to avoid bad situations of unnecessary backlog due to multiple points reappearing across recall checks due to being under different topics. However, this chipped away at the primary design philosophy of being able to defer organization for later in exchange for the ability to quickly jot down important points during a lecture and then organize them later. In hindsight, this restriction was me losing the plot of my app due to thinking about features instead of the bad situations I wanted to mitigate. I wanted to mitigate the annoyance of flashcard generation by allowing the deferment of organization.
Categorizing was not removed, however, since I still believed that placing an idea into one chunk is a useful learning strategy. Instead, points are automatically within the unsorted topic, and can be moved from that topic to another topic later. 

### Adding history to RecallChecking
Initially, RecallChecking did not store attempts in its state. In hindsight, this was a result of me misunderstanding the purpose of RecallChecking as a concept in an attemmpt to make things as modular/simple as possible. Without storing of attempts, RecallChecking can't fulfill the important purpose of being able to compare one's current understanding to what they understood on a previous day. The addition of tracking attempts was necessary to realize the course of beahvior I had in mind which was the ability to compare one's understanding of the prompt they made on a later date.

### Addition of Sharing
As a result of focusing this app too much on my wants and needs, I neglected to add any form of user collaboration to the app until it was too late to add one of much substance. Currently, sharing allows a user to copy a snapshot of another user's prompt so that they can review it themselves later. However, I think this goes against some of the convictions I set forward in my initial problem framing, which was that under most circumstances, creating one's own points and an organizational system for them is an important part of the learning process. I think a more ideal version of the app would have a collaboration feature more focused on faciliating a group recall session. I did not implement such a thing because I was too focused on how difficult it would be to align such a feature with spaced repetition scheduling, as well as the difficulty of testing. Regardless, I think it would've been something great to go for in hindsight, because I don't see many spaced-repetition apps with collaborative review that aren't focused on the classroom setting.

## Changes to visual design from P2
### Search to Create
With topics shifting to being an organizational scheme instead of the unit of review, I reconsidered the P2 UI (admittedly and obviously AI-generated) and shifted to something closer to my paper P1 UI sketches, which featured an infinite scroll of points as the main screen, with buttons to go to scheduling or topics making. While I preferred this UI, there were issues from a UX point of view. Mainly, it was annoying that to do practically anything, you had to go to a separate screen. Editing a card? Separate screen. Making a card? Separate screen. Making a topic? You guessed it, another screen. This made it harder to jot down points while following along with a lecture to review later, and was too many button clicks. Going into P3, I focused on the ability to create points and topics from the main screen by allowing users to create points/topics in the search bar of the main screen.
