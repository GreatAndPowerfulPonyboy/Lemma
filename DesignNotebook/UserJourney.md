### Matthew's journey with Lemma
Matthew is a 3rd-year student studying linear algebra whose recent exam performance has left much to be desired.The worst part was he strided into the exam confidently because he had studied all of the proofs using flashcards. As an experienced flashcard user, he had great faith in the typical spaced repetition system and knew how to tweak things, but it was his first time doing so for such a technical subject.
With some geekery, he manufactured them such that the proof's title was on the frontand when he clicked on the back he walked through a series of increasingly granular steps to describe parts of the proof. Writing all of that for every card took a lot of time, and reviewing them was quite awkward. It was difficult to have his jerry-rigged solution interact well with the system scheduling to allow him to schedule different parts of a proof at different times and also have them grouped together nicely.
Despite these difficulties, he pushed through because he believed setting up an oral-exam type system would help him learn better, and he was convinced that it was working yet his grade clearly demonstrated otherwise.
For a moment, he wondered if he was just stupid since he put in what was for him extraordinary effort, and got poor marks regardless. After commiserating with his friends, they told him that his cards were too detailed, and they didn't use cards like that at all. They would just pull out their notes and a blank page, set a 15 minute timer, and try to regurgitate and re-explain the proof from memory. After getting stuck, they would review what they missed from their notes. Matthew's friends performed similarly or better to him, but they did note
that at times their system was guesswork, and they didn't always know what they had to review if there weren't ample practice exams. Matthew also knew that as a person, he was a fair bit disorganized, which was part of why he used spaced repetition systems in the first place: they handled keeping track of what he needed to review, so he could focus on just reviewing them. Seeking a way to combine these two systems, he did some web scrounging and discovered Lemma, which promised to make setting up a system similar to Matthew's painless, so he could focus
instead on the far more relevant pain of recalling material.
Opening Lemma, he's greeted with a pretty barebones screen with a variety of searches, dropdowns, and buttons at the top and empty placeholders where his supposed flashcards would be. He notices that he didn't start with some beginning 'deck' of cards, and guesses from the topic and tags dropdown that that's the preferred organizational system.
He clicks on add prompt, sending him to a screen (Fig 7) where he's told to add a brief cue for himself and attach it to a topic and one or more tags. A brief popup advises him that topics should be things like 'proof of spectral theorem' and the cues should cover various subsections. He notices that, unlike other systems, he's not encouraged to give the cue an answer immediately. Another brief window advises doing a recall session on the cue before writing an answer.
To test things out, he creates a couple of cues under the topic 'Proof of Spectral Theorem', and returns to the main page. He sees his cues organized by date of creation, and notices that he can now search by topic using the dropdown, though it isn't very relevant right now.
More importantly, the review scheduling button on the main page is no longer greyed out, and by clicking on it he is led to a screen laying out the topics he should review (Fig 5) and the points underneath them. All of the poitns underneath the 'Proof of Spectral Theorem' topic are marked as due for review, and on clicking the review button on the side of the topic he's led to a screen telling him to name his review session and to check off hte points he wants to commit to recalling (Fig 2).
After he checks off the points he commits to recalling, everything fades away and he's presented with a simple recording screen with the topic's name front and center. His browser prompts him to allow microphone, and he's assured that the data is stored only briefly to perform pattern matching on his cues.
After he finishes recording, he's presented with a screen that demarcates which points he did and didn't cover (Fig 4). He notices he cna adjust which points he considers covered or uncovered properly, and leaves the screen.
Fumbling with the UI a bit, Matthew discovers that he can right click on the prompts in the mian page to convert them to flashcards directly, and can also trigger a recall session on one or more cards at once and group them under some tag.
From what he can tell, this seems like a more faithful translation of his friends' method of studying, so he makes a couple more topics and cards and does some reviews to trigger the review scheduling for later.
After about a week, Matthew notices that the Suggested Cards button on the main screen is no longer grey. Upon clicking, he sees a screen notifying him that certain points under his topics have been missed more than three times, so it may be useful to turn them into flashcards for direct studying. (Fig 6)
He notices that the converted cards can be flipped or recalled on individually, while cues are always recalled as a part of their topic.
Matthew uses this system for a bit, and notices that during his free recall sessions he stumbles into a lot more errors than he did while reviewing his flashcards. Being explicitly prompted to explain how specific things connect out loud reveals more gaps in his understanding than he thought he had, which he then leverages into review during Office Hours.
He also spends less time directly creating flashcards, because he can more explicitly make the choice on whether a piece of information needs to be reviewed in isolation or only as part of explaining a larger whole, which gives him more time for reviewing. By the time of the next exam, he's less confident than before, because he's found many gaps in his understanding,
but paradoxically (to him, at least) performs better. The exam hit him with some tricky problems, but he was more prepared to reason through them because he had found and worked through more gaps in the fundamentals.
