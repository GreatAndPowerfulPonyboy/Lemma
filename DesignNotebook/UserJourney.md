### Matthew's journey with Lemma
Matthew is a 3rd-year student studying linear algebra whose recent exam performance has left much to be desired.The worst part was he strided into the exam confidently because he had studied all of the proofs using flashcards. As an experienced flashcard user, he had great faith in the typical spaced repetition system and knew how to tweak things, but it was his first time doing so for such a technical subject.
With some geekery, he manufactured them such that the proof's title was on the frontand when he clicked on the back he walked through a series of increasingly granular steps to describe parts of the proof. Writing all of that for every card took a lot of time, and reviewing them was quite awkward. It was difficult to have his jerry-rigged solution interact well with the system scheduling to allow him to schedule different parts of a proof at different times and also have them grouped together nicely.
Despite these difficulties, he pushed through because he believed setting up an oral-exam type system would help him learn better, and he was convinced that it was working yet his grade clearly demonstrated otherwise.
For a moment, he wondered if he was just stupid since he put in what was for him extraordinary effort, and got poor marks regardless. After commiserating with his friends, they told him that his cards were too detailed, and they didn't use cards like that at all. They would just pull out their notes and a blank page, set a 15 minute timer, and try to regurgitate and re-explain the proof from memory. After getting stuck, they would review what they missed from their notes. Matthew's friends performed similarly or better to him, but they did note
that at times their system was guesswork, and they didn't always know what they had to review if there weren't ample practice exams. Matthew also knew that as a person, he was a fair bit disorganized, which was part of why he used spaced repetition systems in the first place: they handled keeping track of what he needed to review, so he could focus on just reviewing them. Seeking a way to combine these two systems, he did some web scrounging and discovered Lemma, which promised to make setting up a system similar to Matthew's painless, so he could focus
instead on the far more relevant pain of recalling material.

Opening Lemma, he’s greeted with a pretty barebones screen: a search box at the top, a row of tabs (Points, Topics, Prompts, Shared, Scheduling, Suggested cards), and an empty panel where his supposed flashcards would be, telling him to type a point into the box above to capture it. He notices that he didn’t start with some beginning ‘deck’ of cards.
He types a brief cue into the box and presses Enter, and it appears as a card, Unsorted. He notices that, unlike other systems, he’s not asked to give the cue an answer, or even a topic. He adds a few cues covering the subsections of the proof, then opens the Topics tab, creates a topic called ‘Proof of Spectral Theorem’, and drags a box around his new cues to move them all into it at once. Back on the Points tab, he sees his cues organized by date of creation, and notices that he can now filter by topic, though it isn’t very relevant right now.
More importantly, he wants to test himself. He selects the cues under ‘Proof of Spectral Theorem’ and chooses Recall selected, which asks him to name the prompt he’ll explain. He writes ‘Explain the proof of the spectral theorem’ (Fig 2).
Everything fades away and he’s presented with a simple screen with his prompt front and center and a blank page beneath it. His cues are hidden, and he types out everything he can remember. The app tells him his explanation never leaves the browser.
After he submits, he’s presented with a screen that demarcates which points he did and didn’t cover (Fig 4), with his explanation beside them. He notices he can adjust which points he considers covered or uncovered properly. When he finishes, it tells him he recalled 3 of 5 points and offers to track the prompt, which he does.
Fumbling with the UI a bit, Matthew discovers that he can open a cue’s menu to write an answer for it directly, turning it into a flashcard he can flip or drill on its own. He makes a couple more topics and prompts, and his tracked prompts start showing up on the Scheduling tab when they’re due for review.
Later in the week, a friend from his class shares her own prompt, ‘Explain the proof of Cauchy–Schwarz’, with him by his username. It appears under his Shared tab, and he copies it into his prompts. Its points land in his Unsorted pile, ready to file away, and his attempts at the copy never touch her schedule.
After about a week, Matthew notices that the Suggested cards tab is no longer empty. Upon clicking, he sees that certain points have been missed in at least three attempts, so it may be useful to turn them into flashcards for direct studying (Fig 6). He writes an answer for each while his notes are open, and from then on the results screen shows those answers beside the points he missed.
He notices that cards can be flipped or drilled individually, while points are always recalled as part of a prompt.
Matthew uses this system for a bit, and notices that during his free recall sessions he stumbles into a lot more errors than he did while reviewing his flashcards. Being explicitly prompted to explain how specific things connect reveals more gaps in his understanding than he thought he had, which he then leverages into review during Office Hours.
He also spends less time directly creating flashcards, because he can more explicitly make the choice on whether a piece of information needs to be reviewed in isolation or only as part of explaining a larger whole, which gives him more time for reviewing. By the time of the next exam, he’s less confident than before, because he’s found many gaps in his understanding, but paradoxically (to him, at least) performs better. The exam hit him with some tricky problems, but he was more prepared to reason through them because he had found and worked through more gaps in the fundamentals.
